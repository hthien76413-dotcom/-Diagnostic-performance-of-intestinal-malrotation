#H1 Discussion

#N Over 13.6 years, routine ultrasound reports for children with surgically confirmed intestinal malrotation rarely recorded the elements to which contemporary ultrasound accuracy is attributed: D3 or the duodenojejunal junction in three of 117 examinations, enteric fluid in two. What they did record was the whirlpool sign, present in about half of all examinations and of the children with operatively confirmed volvulus. Such low documentation might be expected with free-text reports and no structured template, as here. Positive reports almost always described a whirlpool (59 of 64).

#N Malrotation or volvulus was named in 54.7% of the surgically confirmed children who underwent ultrasound. The 2025 multicenter series, which excluded non-diagnostic and equivocal examinations, reported sensitivities of 93–97% for malrotation and 97% for volvulus [13], and a meta-analysis found a pooled sensitivity of 94% [11], both in children investigated for suspicion. In a multi-institutional cohort, 25.7% of examinations for malrotation were non-diagnostic on the original report [28]. These figures are not interchangeable: ours is conditioned on confirmed disease and on the indications that selected these children, theirs on those exclusions, so the difference combines case mix, examination quality and technique in proportions this audit cannot separate. What the audit does show is that the elements to which published performance is attributed were almost never documented here: D3 [16,17], the most accurate sign for malrotation in the 2025 series [13]; the duodenojejunal junction [12,15]; and enteric fluid, which was associated with better performance in the meta-analysis [11].

#N The temporal analysis is exploratory: the crude rise was imprecise, the booking indicator was chosen after inspecting the data, and booking may reflect clinical suspicion, which was not recorded and may itself relate to finding a whirlpool. Descriptively, whirlpool reporting rose, whereas too few examinations documented the landmarks to show a trend.

#N Two features of the design make the between-modality differences uninterpretable as accuracy. The reference standard is not independent of the index tests: the surgeon presumably knew the preoperative imaging, and a positive UGI series (the last preoperative test in 75.0% of children who had it with another index test) may often have precipitated the operation that confirmed the diagnosis, an incorporation bias asymmetrical between tests. The modalities were also used in different children for different reasons (Table 1), which adjustment cannot correct.

#N The low documentation may bear on departments adopting an ultrasound-first pathway based on sensitivities of 93–97%, and on revisions of the 2020 American College of Radiology guidance [19]: published performance may not apply where examinations differ from those that produced it. Unlike accuracy, documentation needs no reference standard and can be audited prospectively in every examination requested for suspected malrotation. Supplement 4 sets out the audited elements as a minimum reporting dataset, with this audit as the baseline for a second cycle. These data cannot estimate the negative predictive value of a routine report.

#H2 Limitations

#N This is not a diagnostic accuracy study, and detection among surgically confirmed children who underwent a test does not transfer to children investigated for suspicion [11,13,14]. The cohort comprises only children who reached an operation, is weighted toward severe neonatal disease, and provides little information on older children with intermittent symptoms, incidental or non-volvulus malrotation, or stable outpatients. The 52 children without an index test were excluded from the denominators (informative missingness), as were 9 whose operation followed earlier malrotation surgery. Excluding children whose operative record states a rotation below 360° changes prevalence from 88.2% to 84.0% without altering any conclusion (Supplement 2).

#N Index tests were classified from report text, not blinded image review, so the study measures reported detection rather than achievable image-based accuracy; a finding seen but not recorded and an inadequate examination reported as normal cannot be distinguished, although the report is what clinicians act on. The certainty tiers were derived from the same text and, unlike the positive–negative label, were not separately adjudicated. Sex was taken as recorded in the medical record, and sex-specific differences in documentation were not examined. Radiation dose could not be captured. This is one audit cycle at one center, so whether the findings generalize, whether a structured report would raise documentation and whether documentation would raise detection are unknown.

#H1 Conclusion

#N In routine ultrasound reports for children with surgically confirmed intestinal malrotation, the duodenal landmarks to which published performance is attributed were rarely documented, and positive reports almost always described a whirlpool. These are report-level detection rates, not sensitivities, and they do not argue against ultrasound-first pathways. Documentation can be audited without a reference standard, and the audit criteria in Supplement 4 provide a minimum reporting dataset for re-audit.
