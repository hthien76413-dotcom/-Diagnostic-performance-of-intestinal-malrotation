#H1 Discussion

#N Over 13.5 years, routine ultrasound reports for children who proved to have intestinal malrotation rarely recorded the duodenal anatomy to which contemporary ultrasound accuracy is attributed: D3 or the duodenojejunal junction in three of 117 examinations, enteric fluid in two. The finding they did record was the whirlpool sign, present in about half of all examinations and of the children in whom operation confirmed volvulus. In this setting a positive report rested almost entirely on a sign of volvulus.

#N Malrotation or volvulus was named in 54.7% of the surgically confirmed children who underwent ultrasound, compared with 93% in the original clinical reports and 97% on blinded review in the 2025 multicenter series, which excluded non-diagnostic and equivocal examinations [13] (in a multi-institutional cohort, 25.7% of examinations for malrotation were non-diagnostic on the original report [25]), and a pooled 94% in meta-analysis [11], both in children investigated for suspicion. These figures are not interchangeable: ours is conditioned on confirmed disease and on the indications that selected these children, theirs on those exclusions, so the difference combines case mix, examination quality and technique in proportions this audit cannot separate. What it does show is that the elements to which published performance is attributed were almost never documented here: D3 [16, 17], the most accurate single sign in the 2025 series [13]; the duodenojejunal junction [12, 15]; and enteric fluid, associated with better performance in meta-analysis [11]. Whether documenting them would have raised detection cannot be tested, as documentation is a lower bound on what was done.

#N The temporal analysis is exploratory. The crude rise in ultrasound detection was itself imprecise, the booking indicator was chosen after inspection of the data, and the earlier era contributed only 38 examinations. Adding the indicator roughly halved the estimated rise, but booking was not randomized: a child suspected of volvulus is both more likely to be booked for a vessel study and more likely to have a whirlpool, so indication and technique cannot be separated. The consistent observation is descriptive: duodenal landmarks were documented no more often in the later era, while whirlpool reporting rose.

#N Three features of the design make the between-modality differences uninterpretable as accuracy. The reference standard is not independent of the index tests: the surgeon knew the preoperative imaging, and a positive UGI series, the last preoperative test in 75.0% of children who had it with another index test, may often have precipitated the operation that confirmed the diagnosis, an incorporation bias asymmetrical between tests. Pathway position therefore also differed. The modalities were also used in different children for different reasons (Table 1), which no adjustment can undo.

#N Departments adopting an ultrasound-first pathway on the basis of sensitivities of 93–97% should first establish that their examinations resemble those that produced them. Here the duodenal landmarks remained largely undocumented throughout the study period. A structured report requiring explicit statements on D3, the duodenojejunal junction and study adequacy would make this gap auditable. These data cannot estimate the negative predictive value of a routine report, but a department whose reports lack these elements should not assume that published values apply to its own.

#H2 Limitations

#N This is not a diagnostic accuracy study: detection among surgically confirmed children who underwent a test does not transfer to children investigated for suspicion [11, 13, 14]. The cohort contains only children who reached an operation, is weighted toward severe neonatal disease, and provides little information on older children with intermittent symptoms, incidental or non-volvulus malrotation, or stable outpatients. The 52 children without an index test were excluded from the denominators, an informative missingness, as were 9 whose operation followed earlier malrotation surgery. Excluding children whose operative record states a rotation below 360° changes prevalence from 88.2% to 84.0% without altering any conclusion (Supplement 2).

#N Index tests were classified from report text rather than blinded image review, so the study measures reported detection rather than achievable image-based accuracy; a finding seen but not recorded and an inadequate examination reported as normal cannot be distinguished, although the report is what clinicians act on. The certainty tiers were derived from the same text and, unlike the positive–negative label, were not separately adjudicated. Radiation dose could not be captured, and whether the deficit at this center generalizes is unknown.

#H1 Take-Home Points

#N • Routine ultrasound reports for children with surgically confirmed malrotation documented the duodenal landmarks emphasized by published series in 2.6% of examinations and reported the diagnosis almost only when a whirlpool sign was present.

#N • A positive routine ultrasound report rested almost entirely on the whirlpool sign, a sign of volvulus, which was recorded in only 59 of 112 children with operatively confirmed volvulus.

#N • These are report-level detection rates among surgically confirmed children, not sensitivities; they establish no specificity, predictive value or ranking of modalities, and are no argument against ultrasound-first pathways.

#N • Before assuming that published ultrasound performance applies locally, departments should audit whether their own reports document the duodenal landmarks; a structured report would make the gap auditable.
