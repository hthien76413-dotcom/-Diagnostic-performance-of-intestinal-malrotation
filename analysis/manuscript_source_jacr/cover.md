#N Dear Editor,

#N We are pleased to submit our Original Article, "Routine Ultrasound Reports for Pediatric Intestinal Malrotation Rarely Document Duodenal Landmarks: A Single-Center Audit," for consideration by the *Journal of the American College of Radiology*.

#N Ultrasound-first pathways for suspected intestinal malrotation are being adopted on the strength of studies reporting sensitivities of 93–97%, obtained when the third portion of the duodenum (D3), the duodenojejunal junction and the mesenteric vessels are assessed under a dedicated protocol. Clinical decisions, however, rest on the routine report issued during unselected care, and whether routine reports contain the elements that produced those figures has not, to our knowledge, been examined at scale. This is a question of clinical practice management, namely the content and quality of routine radiology reports, rather than of the intrinsic accuracy of a modality, which is why we are submitting it to *JACR*.

#N We audited 13.5 years of routine reports at a tertiary children's hospital: 723 preoperative index examinations in 398 children with surgically confirmed malrotation, coding each examination both for whether it named the diagnosis and for the technical elements it documented, the ultrasound elements independently by an ultrasound physician and a pediatric surgeon. **Only three of 117 ultrasound examinations (2.6%) documented D3 or the duodenojejunal junction**; the superior mesenteric artery–vein relationship was stated in three, enteric fluid administration was recorded twice, and a whirlpool sign was reported in 50.4%. Detection followed that content closely: 5 of the 58 examinations without a reported whirlpool named the diagnosis, and a whirlpool was reported in only 59 of the 112 children in whom operation confirmed midgut volvulus. In routine practice, ultrasound for malrotation functioned as an incomplete test for midgut volvulus rather than as a test for the rotational anomaly itself.

#N Ultrasound detection rose from 42% to 61% across the study period, an imprecise difference that tracked how the examination was booked rather than what it documented: adjusting for a shift toward sessions that included an abdominal great-vessel study reduced it from +18.7 to +8.4 percentage points. By the department's account there was no change of equipment, staffing or protocol over the period, and growing awareness attached to the whirlpool sign rather than to duodenal anatomy, so detection rose while duodenal documentation did not.

#N For radiology departments the implication is practical. Before assuming that published performance applies locally, a department can audit whether its own reports document the duodenal landmarks, and a structured malrotation ultrasound report requiring explicit statements on D3, the duodenojejunal junction and study adequacy would make such a deficit visible prospectively. The audit method, a transparent rule-based classification of free-text reports with negation handling and blinded adjudication, is provided in full with a runnable implementation, so that it can be applied wherever reports are stored electronically.

#N We are explicit about what the design cannot support. The cohort consists only of surgically confirmed cases, so the study estimates report-level detection and not sensitivity; specificity, predictive values and any ranking of ultrasound, CT and the upper gastrointestinal series are neither estimable nor claimed. We have used the term "detection rate" throughout, followed STROBE rather than STARD (checklist enclosed), displayed the confounding by indication rather than adjusting it away, and stated that these data are no argument against ultrasound-first pathways.
