#T Routine Ultrasound Reports for Pediatric Intestinal Malrotation Rarely Document Duodenal Landmarks: A Single-Center Audit

#N [Masked manuscript. Author names, affiliations and corresponding-author details are provided on the separate title page.]

#N Word count: 2,998 (Introduction through Take-Home Points; excluding abstract, references, tables and figure legends); Tables: 4; Figures: 3; Supplemental material: Supplements 1–4

#H1 Abstract

#N **Objective:** Ultrasound is increasingly advocated as the first-line test for suspected intestinal malrotation, based on reported sensitivities of 93–97%. We audited what routine reports documented and whether they named the diagnosis.

#N **Methods:** Retrospective case-only report audit at one tertiary children's hospital (December 2012–June 2026). The preoperative examination closest to operation was classified by a rule-based algorithm with blinded surgical adjudication, and separately audited for documented technical content (ultrasound by two independent readers). Because the cohort was surgically confirmed, the outcome is report-level detection among tested children, with Wilson 95% CIs; specificity, predictive values and comparative accuracy are not estimable.

#N **Results:** Among 450 children (volvulus in 88.2%), 398 underwent at least one index test. Three of 117 ultrasound examinations (2.6%, 95% CI 0.9–7.3) documented the third portion of the duodenum or the duodenojejunal junction, 3 (2.6%) the mesenteric artery–vein relationship and 2 (1.7%) enteric fluid; a whirlpool sign was reported in 50.4%. Ultrasound named malrotation or volvulus in 64 of 117 examinations (54.7%, 45.7–63.4): in all 59 recording a whirlpool, a positivity criterion, and in 5 of 58 without one. A whirlpool was recorded in 59 of the 112 ultrasound-examined children with operatively confirmed volvulus. Detection was 53.8% counting only conclusions naming malrotation, 25.6% excluding tentative wording.

#N **Discussion:** Positive routine ultrasound reports almost always described a whirlpool, and the duodenal landmarks to which published performance is attributed were rarely documented. Documentation, unlike accuracy, can be audited without a reference standard; we provide the criteria as a minimum reporting dataset.

#N **Keywords:** Intestinal malrotation; Intestinal volvulus; Infant, Newborn; Ultrasonography; Radiology report

#H1 Introduction

#N Intestinal malrotation predisposes to midgut volvulus, which can progress within hours to bowel necrosis [1] and lead to short-bowel syndrome or death [1, 2]. Bilious vomiting in a neonate is a surgical emergency until malrotation with volvulus has been excluded, although older children may present atypically and be diagnosed late [3, 4].

#N The upper gastrointestinal (UGI) contrast series has long been the reference imaging test, based on the position of the duodenojejunal junction and the course of the proximal small bowel [5–7], despite well-described mimics [8]. Abdominal CT is not a dedicated test, and the mesenteric swirl it may show is neither reliably present nor specific in children [9, 10]. The field has moved toward ultrasound: series report sensitivities of 93–97%, a systematic review of 17 studies and 2,257 children estimated a pooled sensitivity of 94% [11–13], and a single-center study of 539 children supported ultrasound as a safe first-line test [14]. This performance is attributed to specific technical elements rather than to the modality itself: the third portion of the duodenum (D3), the duodenojejunal junction, the superior mesenteric artery–vein relationship, enteric fluid and dynamic graded compression [12, 13, 15–17]. The same literature records these elements as demanding and not universally achievable, and D3 position alone as insufficient for diagnosis [18]. The 2020 ACR Appropriateness Criteria, which predate most of this evidence, rate ultrasound as "may be appropriate" and the UGI series as "usually appropriate" for bilious vomiting in infants older than 2 days [19].

#N A report audit of the UGI series for malrotation found discrepancies with blinded re-reading and proposed a structured template [20], but whether routine reports from a general pediatric ultrasound service document the elements to which that performance is attributed, and how this relates to whether the diagnosis is reported, has not to our knowledge been examined at scale.

#N We therefore audited 13.6 years of routine reports at a tertiary children's hospital. Because every child had surgically confirmed malrotation, the study measures how often the disease was reported and with what documented content, not how accurate the tests are. The UGI series and CT are reported alongside ultrasound to show where ultrasound sat in the diagnostic pathway, not to rank them. The audit criteria are provided as a minimum reporting dataset (Supplement 4).

#H1 Methods

#H2 Design, terminology and estimable quantities

#N This was a retrospective, single-center, case-only report audit approved by the institutional review board with waiver of consent. Reporting follows STROBE [21]; STARD was not used, as the design is not that of a diagnostic accuracy study. The cohort includes no children in whom malrotation was suspected and excluded. The outcome is the **report-level detection rate among surgically confirmed children who underwent the test**. We avoid the term "sensitivity", which does not transfer to children investigated for suspicion. Specificity, predictive values and any accuracy-based ranking cannot be estimated. The whirlpool sign is a sign of volvulus rather than of malrotation [22].

#H2 Participants, reference standard and index tests

#N The institutional clinical research database supplied the records of 711 children with a recorded diagnosis of intestinal malrotation between December 2012 and June 2026, including 559 operative records in 499 children. Every operative record was read, and operations whose operative diagnosis named intestinal malrotation or whose procedure was recorded as a Ladd procedure were selected (search strings in Supplement 1): 503 records in 465 children. The anchor operation was each child's first such operation. One author, with the imaging labels visible, re-read all operative records for 34 children whose anchor operation was not named as a malrotation procedure or followed earlier malrotation surgery: 19 were retained and 15 excluded, 6 in whom that operation did not confirm malrotation and 9 reoperations (Supplement 1). The remaining 450 consecutive children form the reference standard. Midgut volvulus was defined by an explicit operative statement of torsion or by a documented degree of midgut or mesenteric rotation, with no minimum degree required (see Limitations). No sample-size calculation was performed.

#N Three modalities were evaluated: ultrasound (sessions booked as gastrointestinal, abdominal great-vessel or pyloric), abdominal CT and the UGI series; contrast enemas were not counted. For each child and modality, the index examination was the examination episode closest to operation, pooling every report of that modality issued that day, as the department routinely issues separate gastrointestinal and great-vessel reports for one ultrasound session. Operative times were recorded as dates only, so the 154 same-day reports and the 27 index examinations more than 7 days before operation were reviewed individually; three issued after the operation or for an unrelated earlier illness were removed. This yielded 723 index examinations from 761 reports; 32 earlier repeat examinations were not audited (Figure 1). The clinical indication was not retrievable, so confounding by indication is displayed in Table 1, not adjusted for.

#N Ultrasound was reported by ultrasound physicians, and CT and the UGI series by radiologists, during routine care, unaware of the operative findings but not blinded to clinical history or previous imaging, and not by a designated reader group. Reports were free text, with no structured malrotation template in use. The department reports no change in ultrasound equipment, reporting staff or protocol during the study period.

#H2 Report-content audit and classification

#N Each ultrasound index examination was coded by two readers, an ultrasound physician and a pediatric surgeon, both authors who had reported none of the examinations, working independently from the findings and conclusion text against written criteria for twelve elements, from D3 and the duodenojejunal junction to the whirlpool sign and limiting bowel gas (Table 3). Technique elements were counted as documented whether the finding was normal or abnormal; the whirlpool sign, a finding, only where described as present. Agreement was assessed with Cohen kappa and disagreements adjudicated. Booking category and bedside performance were taken from the examination name, and CT and UGI landmarks were coded by text patterns (Supplement 1).

#N An examination was classified as positive when its conclusion named malrotation or midgut volvulus, including tentative phrasing, or described a modality-specific sign: for ultrasound, a whirlpool or an explicit statement of mesenteric vessel inversion; for the UGI series, a corkscrew or spring appearance or a malpositioned duodenojejunal junction; for CT, a mesenteric whirl or an abnormal duodenal position. Explicit negation was classified as negative, and isolated gastric volvulus was not counted. Because the whirlpool is both an audited element and a positivity criterion, and a conclusion may name volvulus without malrotation, detection was also computed counting only conclusions that named malrotation. Tentative phrasing is standard here, so the certainty tier of every positive conclusion was tabulated and the analyses repeated with "possible" tiers counted as negative.

#N Report text was classified using a rule-based, clause-level algorithm with explicit negation handling [23] (Supplement 1). A pediatric surgeon, one of the authors, blinded to the operative findings adjudicated 32 reports: agreement was 92% (Cohen kappa 0.83, 95% CI 0.61–1.00) in a stratified random sample of 24, and a targeted review of 8 suspected discordances found 5 further under-calls, excluded from the agreement statistics. A two-directional screen of every index examination then returned nine positives that no diagnostic term or sign pattern supported, three of which source review confirmed as over-calls. All errors were corrected; because adjudication read the whole report, the final label is the algorithmic label after review, not the conclusion alone. With a single adjudicator, interobserver agreement on the detection label could not be estimated.

#H2 Statistical analysis

#N Detection rates are presented with denominators and Wilson 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Between-modality comparison used a generalized estimating equation (GEE) logistic model [24] with an exchangeable working correlation, patient-level clustering and cluster-robust standard errors, unadjusted and adjusted for era (2012–2018 vs 2019–2026) and age group (≤28 days, 29 days–1 year, >1 year), with standardized detection and cluster-bootstrap CIs. Modalities a child did not undergo are unobserved outcomes, not missing at random here, so these coefficients describe reporting among children who underwent each test. A pre-specified modality-by-volvulus interaction was not estimable, no ultrasound examination being positive among the five children without volvulus; a Firth penalized estimate is given in Supplement 2 [25, 26]. In the subgroup that underwent all three modalities, detection was compared using the Cochran Q test, the exact McNemar test and paired differences with bootstrap CIs (Supplement 2). The temporal change was explored, after inspection of the data, using modality-specific logistic models of detection on era before and after adding one covariate (booking category or contrast enhancement), reported as odds ratios and average marginal effects (Table 4); the era boundary was varied from 2019 to 2022 and era-by-covariate interactions fitted. Sensitivity analyses varied the outcome definition, index examination, interval to operation and ultrasound denominator (Supplement 2). P values were not adjusted for multiplicity; Supplement 2 lists which analyses were pre-specified. Analyses used Python 3.11 (statsmodels 0.15, SciPy 1.17).
