Abstract

Objective: Ultrasound is increasingly advocated as the first-line test for suspected intestinal malrotation, based on reported sensitivities of 93–97%. We audited what routine reports documented and whether they named the diagnosis.

Methods: Retrospective case-only report audit at one tertiary children's hospital (December 2012–June 2026). The preoperative examination closest to operation was classified by a rule-based algorithm with blinded surgical adjudication, and separately audited for documented technical content (ultrasound by two independent readers). Because the cohort was surgically confirmed, the outcome is report-level detection among tested children, with Wilson 95% CIs; specificity, predictive values and comparative accuracy are not estimable.

Results: Among 450 children (volvulus in 88.2%), 398 underwent at least one index test. Three of 117 ultrasound examinations (2.6%, 95% CI 0.9–7.3) documented the third portion of the duodenum or the duodenojejunal junction, 3 (2.6%) the mesenteric artery–vein relationship and 2 (1.7%) enteric fluid; a whirlpool sign was reported in 50.4%. Ultrasound named malrotation or volvulus in 64 of 117 examinations (54.7%, 45.7–63.4): in all 59 recording a whirlpool, itself a positivity criterion, and in 5 of 58 without one. A whirlpool was recorded in 59 of 112 children with operatively confirmed volvulus. Detection was 53.8% counting only conclusions naming malrotation, 25.6% excluding tentative wording.

Discussion: Positive routine ultrasound reports rested almost entirely on the whirlpool sign, and the duodenal landmarks to which published performance is attributed were rarely documented. Documentation, unlike accuracy, can be audited without a reference standard; we provide the criteria as a minimum reporting dataset.

Keywords: Intestinal malrotation; Intestinal volvulus; Infant, Newborn; Ultrasonography; Radiology report
