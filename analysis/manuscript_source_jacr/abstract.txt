Abstract

Objective: Ultrasound is increasingly advocated as the first-line test for suspected intestinal malrotation, based on reported sensitivities of 93–97%. We audited what routine reports document and whether the diagnosis was reported.

Methods: Retrospective case-only report audit at one tertiary children's hospital (December 2012–June 2026). Preoperative examination episodes closest to operation were classified for detection by a rule-based algorithm with blinded surgical adjudication, and separately audited for documented technical content (ultrasound by two independent readers). Because the cohort was surgically confirmed, the outcome is report-level detection among children who underwent the test; specificity, predictive values and comparative accuracy are not estimable.

Results: Among 450 children (volvulus in 88.2%), 398 underwent at least one index test. Three of 117 ultrasound examinations (2.6%, 95% CI 0.9–7.3) documented the third portion of the duodenum or the duodenojejunal junction, 3 (2.6%) the mesenteric artery–vein relationship and 2 (1.7%) enteric fluid; a whirlpool sign was reported in 50.4%. Ultrasound named malrotation or volvulus in 64 of 117 examinations (54.7%): in all 59 recording a whirlpool, itself a positivity criterion, and in 5 of 58 without one. A whirlpool was recorded in 59 of 112 children with operatively confirmed volvulus. Counting only conclusions naming malrotation, ultrasound detection was 53.8%.

Discussion: Positive routine ultrasound reports rested almost entirely on the whirlpool sign, and the duodenal landmarks to which published performance is attributed were rarely documented. Whether documenting them would close the gap cannot be tested from reports, but their absence can be audited.

Keywords: Intestinal malrotation; Intestinal volvulus; Infant, Newborn; Ultrasonography; Radiology report
