#T Response to the Section Editor and Reviewers

#N Manuscript: "Real-world detection rates of gastrointestinal ultrasound, abdominal CT, and the upper gastrointestinal contrast series for intestinal malrotation in children: a 13.5-year single-center cohort" (Submission ID 0a307e19-d139-4e4c-865e-36c1b10f0b06)

#N Revised title: "Routine ultrasound reports for intestinal malrotation rarely document duodenal landmarks: a report-level audit of 740 preoperative examinations in 465 surgically confirmed children"

#N We are grateful to the Section Editor and both reviewers for an unusually clear diagnosis of what was wrong with the manuscript. The three sets of comments converge on one conclusion, and we now accept it without reservation: the study we submitted was framed as a comparative diagnostic accuracy study, and the design cannot support that framing. Rather than defend the original framing, we have rebuilt the paper around the question the data can answer, and around the finding that Reviewer 1 correctly identified as the real novelty — that routine ultrasound underperforms the literature — with new analyses that identify why.

#N Four changes are structural rather than cosmetic:

#N **1. The study is no longer presented as a diagnostic accuracy study.** The word "sensitivity" has been removed throughout and replaced by "report-level detection rate among surgically confirmed cases". A dedicated Methods subsection ("Design, terminology and what this study can and cannot estimate") states explicitly that specificity, predictive values and any ranking of the three modalities are not estimable. STARD is explicitly not claimed. The title, abstract, results and conclusions have all been rewritten accordingly.

#N **2. The centre of gravity has moved to the ultrasound finding, supported by a new report-content audit.** We audited all 119 ultrasound index reports for the technical elements that drive published ultrasound accuracy. The result is stark and, we believe, the most useful thing in the paper: **not one report (0/119) documented the third portion of the duodenum or the duodenojejunal junction**; 8.4% described the superior mesenteric artery–vein relationship; one recorded enteric fluid. Detection tracked content almost deterministically — 100% (59/59) when a whirlpool sign was recorded, 10% (6/60) when it was not. Routine ultrasound at our institution was a test for volvulus, not for malrotation, and this explains the gap to the published 93–97% far better than any statement about "real-world performance".

#N **3. The temporal improvement has been investigated rather than used as an excuse.** Reviewer 2 suggested this could be one of the most valuable findings, and we agree. Ultrasound detection rose from 41.0% to 61.3% between eras. In a mediation-style analysis, the era effect (OR 2.27, 95% CI 1.04–4.96) fell to a non-significant 1.56 (0.67–3.62) once we adjusted for a documented shift toward mesenteric-vessel-focused examination (OR 3.46, 1.56–7.67), and detection with a vessel-focused study was ~70% in both eras. The department improved because the examination changed, not because time passed.

#N **4. Every claim of superiority has been withdrawn.** The comparison of contrast-enhanced CT with the UGI series has been removed from the abstract. The paired analysis is presented as a selected subgroup, not as confirmation. The between-modality models are explicitly labelled as descriptions of indication-driven practice.

#N The detailed responses follow. Reviewer comments are in quotation marks; our responses follow each.

#H1 Section Editor (Dr Kushaljit Singh Sodhi)

#H2 SE1. "The major concern: this is not really a diagnostic accuracy study… the denominators are fundamentally different. You cannot conclude UGI is more accurate than US or CT."

#N We accept this entirely, and it is now the organising principle of the manuscript rather than a caveat within it.

#N Concretely: (a) the title no longer refers to detection rates of three modalities in a way that implies comparison, and instead names the report-audit finding; (b) "sensitivity" has been eliminated as a term for our proportions; (c) a Methods subsection states that the three rates rest on different, indication-selected denominators and are not comparable; (d) Fig. 3 now carries the denominator of each estimate in bold in the axis label and a warning in the figure title that the estimates are not sensitivities and must not be compared with one another; (e) the GEE model is described as a summary of indication-driven detection whose modality coefficients absorb indication, pathway position and examination content; and (f) the Conclusion states that the study does not rank the three modalities and should not be used to argue against ultrasound-first pathways.

#N We have also added the evidence that makes the non-comparability concrete rather than asserted. New Table 1 shows the characteristics of the children who received each test; new results show that CT was the first index test in 183 of 271 multiply-imaged children (67.5%) and the UGI series the last before operation in 184 (67.9%), and that UGI detection was higher in that terminal position (80.4%) than earlier in the pathway (70.5%). The UGI series was the confirmatory test in our pathway, which is a property of the pathway, not of the test.

#H2 SE2. "The 59-patient paired cohort does not solve the problem… Why did these 59 undergo all three? They are almost certainly the diagnostically difficult patients… Furthermore, 8/59 had examinations separated by >2 days."

#N Agreed on both points, and we have acted on both.

#N On selection: we have quantified it rather than merely conceding it. The 59 differ sharply from the other 351 imaged children — 96.6% versus 86.6% volvulus, 83.1% versus 65.5% neonates, 62.7% versus 31.3% operated in the later era. The manuscript now states that the question this subgroup answers is "among children investigated intensively enough to receive all three tests, which examination named the diagnosis most often", and that this is not a population-level comparison of sensitivity. The word "confirmed" has been removed from every description of this analysis, including the abstract.

#N On timing: we recomputed the interval between the first and last of the three examinations using exact timestamps (median 0.9 days, IQR 0.6–1.6); 47 of 59 (79.7%) were within 48 h and 31 (52.5%) within 24 h. We then repeated the entire paired analysis in both windows (new Table 5, new Fig. 2). Within 48 h detection was 83.0%, 59.6% and 48.9% for the UGI series, ultrasound and CT (Cochran's Q p<0.001); within 24 h, 87.1%, 64.5% and 51.6% (p=0.002). Restricting to near-simultaneous examinations does not weaken the pattern, which addresses the possibility that volvulus evolved between studies — but we state explicitly that no timing restriction can undo the selection that defines the subgroup.

#H2 SE3. "The study population is highly skewed toward severe disease… tells us very little about older children, intermittent vomiting, incidental malrotation, non-volvulus malrotation, equivocal cases, outpatient presentations, stable children — precisely the populations in which the modern US-first versus UGI debate is most relevant."

#N Accepted without qualification. The manuscript now states this in three places rather than one, and quantifies it: 302 of 465 children (64.9%) were neonates, 403 (86.7%) had volvulus, and only 62 (13.3%) had malrotation without volvulus, of whom just 6 underwent ultrasound. A dedicated Results paragraph ("Age, and the populations this cohort does not describe") reports detection by age category and states that the cohort contains very little information about the populations the Editor lists and that we make no claims about them. The Discussion states that the study does not resolve the ultrasound-first debate, and the Conclusion repeats that these findings should not be used to argue against ultrasound-first pathways.

#N We would add one point in explanation, not defence: because the study is now a report audit rather than an accuracy study, this narrow population is a limitation on generalisability of the report-content finding rather than a source of bias in it. The observation that 0 of 119 ultrasound reports documented D3 does not depend on the case mix; what depends on case mix is whether other departments would show the same deficit, which we identify as the question most worth asking next.

#H1 Reviewer 1

#H2 R1.1. "Did 55 children undergo surgery without any preoperative imaging? This clearly defies real-world clinical logic."

#N The reviewer is right that this would defy clinical logic, and we apologise for wording that implied it. It was a reporting failure on our part, not a description of practice. Those 55 children had no preoperative examination of the **three index modalities**; they were not operated on without imaging.

#N We have returned to the source records and established exactly what they received. Of the 55: 46 had preoperative radiographs (44 including the abdomen); 12 had a contrast enema of the colon (which we did not count as a UGI series); 22 had ultrasound of another region; 7 had CT of another region. Only 5 had no in-hospital preoperative study of any type — and the admission record of every one of those 5 documents imaging performed before transfer, in three cases already reporting malrotation or volvulus. Across all 55, prior outside or outpatient imaging was documented in 33, and had already reported malrotation or volvulus in 19.

#N These children were therefore operated on after the diagnosis had been made or strongly suggested elsewhere, or after a radiograph and contrast enema in an era when that combination was more often considered sufficient. This is now stated in the Results, and the flow diagram (Fig. 1) has been redrawn to show the breakdown explicitly. We also show that they differed systematically from the imaged children (older, less often neonates, less often bilious vomiting, less often volvulus), and describe their exclusion as informative missingness.

#H2 R1.2. "The authors acknowledge that negative ultrasound should not be considered sufficient to exclude uncomplicated malrotation, yet the abstract conclusion still emphasises that UGI is highest. These have completely different clinical implications — a contradiction."

#N Agreed. The abstract conclusion no longer says that the UGI series was highest. It now reads:

#N *"In routine practice, ultrasound for malrotation was performed and reported as a whirlpool-dependent test for volvulus rather than as a protocolised assessment of duodenal position, and its detection tracked that content almost perfectly. The gap between real-world and published ultrasound performance is best explained by examination technique and reporting content, not by any intrinsic limitation of ultrasound. These are detection rates in children with confirmed disease and do not rank the accuracy of the three modalities."*

#N The abstract and the Discussion now carry the same message, and that message is about examination content rather than about a ranking of modalities.

#H2 R1.3. "The finding that contrast-enhanced CT is comparable to UGI is presented as important, yet the indications were confounded and non-randomised. If the comparison is unreliable, why is it in the abstract and the main results?"

#N A fair objection, and we have removed it from the abstract. Enhanced-versus-unenhanced CT is now reported once, within Table 2 as an explicitly labelled exploratory subgroup, and discussed once, where we state that the association of enhancement with detection (84.6% vs 47.4%) is consistent with vascular opacification being what makes the whirl visible, but that enhanced studies were selected by indication and performed more often in older children, so the contrast is confounded and hypothesis-generating. We also say in the Discussion, in terms, that a confounded subgroup contrast does not belong among a study's headline results.

#H2 R1.4. "The authors repeatedly emphasise that UGI has the highest detection rate. This is already well known… The core of your article should be that ultrasound, in daily practice, is far less excellent than reported in the literature. That is the key novelty. Please do not confuse the primary and secondary points."

#N This comment reshaped the paper, and we thank the reviewer for it. It is now the paper's primary point, and we have gone further than restating it: we have measured *why*.

#N Specifically, we added a report-content audit of all 740 index examinations. For ultrasound (new Table 3, new Fig. 4): D3 or the duodenojejunal junction documented in 0 of 119 reports; the SMA–SMV relationship in 8.4%; enteric fluid in 0.8%; explicit vessel inversion in 0.8% (although vessel inversion was one of our two positivity criteria); a whirlpool in 49.6%. Detection was 100% (59/59) when a whirlpool was recorded and 10% (6/60) when it was not, and 100% (10/10) when the vessel relationship was described.

#N The interpretation now runs: routine ultrasound in our department was, in practice, a test for midgut volvulus; a child whose midgut was not actively twisted at the time of scanning was unlikely to be identified; and the distance from the published 93–97% is a distance in examination technique and reporting, not a property of ultrasound. The UGI series' higher detection is explained in the same terms — it was the only modality whose routine reports documented the defining landmark (the duodenojejunal junction) in the majority of examinations — rather than asserted as superiority.

#H2 R1.5. "Regarding surgeons being aware of the preoperative imaging results, given the well-known high specificity of UGI, could this have introduced bias? Could surgeons have been subjectively influenced to believe that surgery was indicated?"

#N Yes. We now answer this directly rather than listing it as a generic limitation. The Discussion states that whether surgeons could have been influenced toward operating by a positive UGI series is "answered in the affirmative — that is how a confirmatory test functions in a diagnostic pathway — and it is a reason why these data cannot rank the tests."

#N We also provide supporting evidence rather than assertion: the UGI series was the last preoperative examination in 184 of 245 children who received it (75.1%), against 19.2% for CT and 36.8% for ultrasound, and its detection was higher in that terminal position (80.4%) than when another examination followed (70.5%). Because the operative diagnosis is our reference standard and the operation was often precipitated by the UGI result, incorporation bias is asymmetrical between modalities and inflates the apparent advantage of whichever test drove the decision to operate. This is now stated as a reason the between-modality comparison cannot be read as an accuracy comparison, not merely as a caveat.

#H2 R2.10. "The temporal improvement in ultrasound performance deserves more attention… Was the technology changed? Were paediatric radiologists introduced? Did the protocol change? Did reporting templates change? Did the use of the whirlpool sign increase? This could be one of the most valuable findings of the paper."

#N We agree, and we pursued it. New Table 6 and a dedicated Results subsection report the following.

#N Ultrasound utilisation among operated children rose from 13% (39/299) to 48% (80/166) between eras, and no gastrointestinal ultrasound in this cohort predates 2016. The recorded examination type shifted markedly: studies recorded as abdominal great-vessel ultrasound rose from 28.2% to 62.5% (Fisher p<0.001) while gastrointestinal ultrasound fell from 74.4% to 48.8%; whirlpool documentation rose from 41.0% to 53.8%, and SMA–SMV documentation from 5.1% to 10.0% (still very low in both eras).

#N The key analysis is a mediation-style model. Later era was associated with detection (OR 2.27, 95% CI 1.04–4.96, p=0.039). Adding the vessel-focused examination indicator reduced the era term to 1.56 (0.67–3.62, p=0.30), while the examination indicator carried an OR of 3.46 (1.56–7.67, p=0.002). Detection with a vessel-focused study was essentially identical in the two eras (72.7% versus 70.0%), whereas without it, it was 28.6% and 46.7%. In other words, the improvement is explained by what was done, not by when it was done.

#N The same analysis for CT shows partial mediation by contrast enhancement (era OR 2.26 falling to 1.98; enhancement OR 5.53), and for the UGI series, whose technique did not change materially, there is no era effect at all (OR 1.38, p=0.30) — an internal consistency check we found reassuring.

#N On the questions we could not answer from the record — scanner replacement, staffing changes, introduction of dedicated paediatric radiologists — we have not speculated.
