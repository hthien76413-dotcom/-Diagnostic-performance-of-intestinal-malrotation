#T Routine ultrasound reports for intestinal malrotation rarely document duodenal landmarks: a report-level audit of 740 preoperative examinations in 465 surgically confirmed children

#N [Author names, affiliations, and corresponding-author details have been removed for double-blind peer review and are provided on the separate title page.]

#N Word count: 5,714 (main text, excluding title, abstract, references, tables and figure legends); Tables: 6; Figures: 4; Supplementary: 3 Online Resources

#H1 Abstract

#N **Background** Ultrasound is increasingly advocated as the first-line test for suspected intestinal malrotation, supported by studies reporting sensitivities of 93–97% when the third portion of the duodenum and the mesenteric vessels are assessed under dedicated protocols. Whether routine, unselected clinical practice reproduces those conditions has not been examined.

#N **Objective** To audit what routine radiology reports for gastrointestinal ultrasound, abdominal CT and the upper gastrointestinal (UGI) contrast series actually document and detect in children with surgically confirmed malrotation, and to identify the report-level features associated with detection.

#N **Materials and methods** Retrospective, case-only report audit at one tertiary children's hospital (December 2012–June 2026). All preoperative index reports of the three modalities were classified for malrotation detection by a rule-based algorithm with surgeon adjudication, and were separately audited for documented technical content (duodenal landmarks, mesenteric-vessel relationship, whirlpool sign, enteric fluid). Because the cohort was assembled on confirmed disease, the outcome is the report-level detection rate among surgically confirmed cases; sensitivity, specificity, predictive values and comparative accuracy are not estimable.

#N **Results** Among 465 children (352 [75.7%] male; median age at operation 13 days; midgut volvulus in 403 [86.7%]), 410 had at least one preoperative index test (740 examinations). No ultrasound report (0/119) documented the third portion of the duodenum or the duodenojejunal junction, 10 (8.4%) described the superior mesenteric artery–vein relationship, and 1 (0.8%) recorded enteric fluid administration; a whirlpool sign was recorded in 59 (49.6%). Ultrasound reported malrotation in 59 of 59 examinations in which a whirlpool was documented and in 6 of 60 in which it was not (100% vs 10%). Report-level detection was 237/301 (78.7%) for the UGI series, 171/320 (53.4%) for CT and 65/119 (54.6%) for ultrasound, on non-comparable, indication-selected denominators. Ultrasound detection rose from 41.0% (2012–2018) to 61.3% (2019–2026); this era effect was abolished by adjustment for a shift toward mesenteric-vessel-focused examination (era odds ratio 2.27 [95% CI 1.04–4.96] falling to 1.56 [0.67–3.62]; protocol odds ratio 3.46 [1.56–7.67]).

#N **Conclusion** In routine practice, ultrasound for malrotation was performed and reported as a whirlpool-dependent test for volvulus rather than as a protocolised assessment of duodenal position, and its detection tracked that content almost perfectly. The gap between real-world and published ultrasound performance is best explained by examination technique and reporting content, not by any intrinsic limitation of ultrasound. These are detection rates in children with confirmed disease and do not rank the accuracy of the three modalities.

#N **Keywords:** Intestinal malrotation; Intestinal volvulus; Infant, Newborn; Ultrasonography; Radiology report; Quality improvement

#H1 Introduction

#N Intestinal malrotation results from incomplete rotation and fixation of the midgut during fetal development and predisposes to midgut volvulus, in which the small bowel twists around a narrow mesenteric pedicle and can progress within hours to bowel ischaemia, necrosis, short-bowel syndrome or death [1, 2]. Bilious vomiting in a neonate remains a surgical emergency until malrotation with volvulus has been excluded, although older infants and children may present with intermittent or atypical symptoms and be diagnosed late [3, 4].

#N The upper gastrointestinal (UGI) contrast series has long been the reference imaging test, based on the position of the duodenojejunal junction and the course of the proximal small bowel [5, 6, 13]. Over the past decade, however, the field has moved substantially toward ultrasound. Contemporary series report ultrasound sensitivities of 93–97% and specificities of 96–100% for malrotation when performed with a dedicated protocol, and a systematic review of 17 studies and 2,257 children estimated a pooled sensitivity of 94% and specificity of 100% [7–9, 12]. A multicentre study of 539 children investigated for clinical suspicion of malrotation supported ultrasound as a safe first-line investigation [19]. Critically, that performance is not attributed to ultrasound as a modality in the abstract, but to specific technical elements: visualisation of the intraperitoneal versus retroperitoneal course of the third portion of the duodenum (D3), assessment of the duodenojejunal junction, the superior mesenteric artery–vein relationship, the use of enteric fluid to distend the duodenum, and dynamic graded compression [9, 12, 18, 20, 21]. The same literature records that these elements are technically demanding, operator-dependent, and not universally achievable — and that D3 position alone has been argued to be insufficient for a confident diagnosis [22].

#N This creates a question those studies cannot answer. They describe what ultrasound achieves when performed and interpreted under favourable conditions, usually by dedicated paediatric sonographers and readers who know that malrotation is the question; what clinicians act on is the routine report issued during unselected care. If the technical elements that drive published accuracy are frequently absent from routine examinations, a hospital adopting an ultrasound-first pathway on the strength of the published figures may not obtain the published performance. To our knowledge, no study has examined the content of routine malrotation ultrasound reports at scale, or linked that content to whether the diagnosis was reported.

#N We therefore performed a report-level audit over 13.5 years at a tertiary children's hospital, to characterise what routine ultrasound, CT and UGI reports document in children who proved at operation to have malrotation, and which documented findings accompanied a positive report. Because every child had surgically confirmed malrotation, the study measures how often the disease was named in the report and why — not how accurate the tests are. This constraint governs every result reported below.

#H1 Materials and methods

#H2 Design, terminology and what this study can and cannot estimate

#N This was a retrospective, single-centre, case-only report audit, approved by the institutional review board with waiver of informed consent. Reporting follows STROBE [14]; STARD was deliberately not used, because the design does not meet the definition of a diagnostic accuracy study.

#N The cohort was assembled exclusively from children with an operative diagnosis of malrotation and therefore contains no children in whom malrotation was suspected and excluded. Three consequences are applied consistently throughout. First, the outcome is the **report-level detection rate among surgically confirmed cases** — the proportion of preoperative index reports whose conclusion raised malrotation. We do not call these proportions "sensitivity", because sensitivity is a property of a test applied to a defined clinical population and the term would invite the inference that the figure applies to children investigated for suspected malrotation. It does not. Second, specificity, predictive values and any accuracy-based ranking of the three modalities are not estimable and are neither reported nor implied. Third, each modality was ordered for different clinical reasons in overlapping but non-identical groups of children, so the three detection rates rest on different, indication-selected denominators; between-modality models describe indication-driven practice, not controlled comparisons of test performance.

#N We distinguish *malrotation* (the rotational anomaly), *midgut volvulus* (an acute twist on the mesenteric pedicle) and *malrotation with volvulus* throughout. The whirlpool sign is a sign of volvulus, not of malrotation in isolation, and the wording of every result preserves that distinction.

#H2 Participants and reference standard

#N We identified consecutive children who underwent surgery for intestinal malrotation between December 2012 and June 2026 from the institutional clinical and surgical records database. Inclusion required an operative diagnosis of malrotation, which served as the reference standard. Midgut volvulus was defined by an explicit operative statement of volvulus or a documented degree of midgut or mesenteric rotation (for example, clockwise rotation 360°–720°); isolated gastric volvulus and torsion of other organs were excluded. No child meeting the criterion was excluded and no sample-size calculation was performed; 465 children constituted the cohort. Age at operation was calculated as age at admission for the operative encounter plus the interval from admission to operation, the operative encounter being identified by linking each operative record to its own admission; the operation date fell within the linked admission for all 465 children.

#H2 Index tests and index-test selection

#N Three preoperative modalities were evaluated: gastrointestinal and mesenteric-vessel ultrasound (examinations coded as gastrointestinal, abdominal great-vessel or pyloric ultrasound), abdominal CT (overall and by contrast enhancement), and the UGI contrast series (upper or whole-gastrointestinal study, water-soluble contrast or barium). Contrast enema studies of the colon were not counted as a UGI series and are reported separately. For each child and modality the preoperative examination closest to the operation was the index test; examinations dated after the operation were excluded.

#N All examinations were performed and reported during routine clinical care by radiologists who were unaware of the eventual operative findings but who were not blinded to the clinical history or to previous imaging. Ultrasound examinations were performed within the general paediatric ultrasound service rather than by a designated malrotation reader group. Reports were free text; no structured malrotation reporting template was in use at any point during the study period, and no formally documented revision of the ultrasound examination protocol could be identified in departmental records ⟦AUTHORS: confirm and, if a protocol or equipment change did occur, give its date⟧. Examination technique therefore could not be reconstructed from a protocol document, and we instead audited the reports themselves for the technical elements that a protocolised malrotation examination would be expected to document (below). The audit is a lower bound on what was performed: an element assessed but not written down is indistinguishable here from one never assessed — a limitation intrinsic to the question, since the report is what the referring clinician acts on. Consistent with recommendations that CT-based studies report radiation dose, we sought per-examination CTDIvol, dose–length product, acquisition parameters and contrast dose; these were not retrievable, because CT was ascertained from archived report text rather than from a structured dose registry spanning 13.5 years (see Limitations). Enhancement status was determined from the examination name and report text.

#H2 Report-content audit

#N Because the central question was what routine reports contain, each index report was separately coded for documented technical content by pre-specified text patterns applied to the findings and conclusion sections. For ultrasound these covered the duodenum in general and the third portion of the duodenum or duodenojejunal junction in particular, the superior mesenteric artery–vein relationship and explicit vessel inversion, a whirlpool or spiral appearance, administration of enteric fluid, an explicit statement that bowel gas limited the study, caecal position, and bedside performance; for CT, contrast enhancement, a mesenteric whirl, duodenal position, the vessel relationship and three-dimensional reconstruction; and for the UGI series, the duodenojejunal junction, a corkscrew proximal small bowel, jejunal position and caecal position. The full pattern dictionary is given in Online Resource 1.

#H2 Classification of reports as positive or negative

#N An examination was classified as positive when its conclusion raised malrotation, including tentative phrasing (suspected, possible, most likely, first consideration, cannot be excluded, recommended to be ruled out) or a modality-appropriate diagnostic sign: on ultrasound a whirlpool sign or inversion of the superior mesenteric artery and vein; on the UGI series an abnormally positioned duodenojejunal junction or a corkscrew course of the proximal small bowel; and on CT a mesenteric whirl or an abnormal duodenal position. Explicit negation (no sign of malrotation, normal rotation) was classified as negative. Isolated benign gastric volvulus was not counted as positive. Technically non-diagnostic studies were not separately coded as indeterminate but were classified by their stated conclusion. Because tentative phrasing is standard in reports from our department and its inclusion is a substantive choice, we (a) tabulated the certainty tier of every positive conclusion (definite, probable, possible) by modality and (b) repeated the principal analyses after reclassifying all "possible"-tier conclusions as negative.

#N Report text was classified with a rule-based, clause-level algorithm with explicit negation handling, informed by general natural language processing approaches in radiology [15]. The complete rule dictionary, negation and scope logic, priority rules, worked examples of the misclassifications encountered, and the per-modality distribution of positive, negative and tentative labels are provided in Online Resource 1. A paediatric surgeon blinded to the operative findings adjudicated 32 reports in two samples. In a stratified random sample of 24 reports (positive and negative machine labels balanced across modalities), algorithm–adjudicator agreement was 92% (22/24; Cohen kappa 0.83; per-modality 88%, 88% and 100% for the UGI series, CT and ultrasound). A separate targeted review of the 8 reports in which a malrotation sign co-occurred with a negative machine label identified 5 further machine under-calls, which were corrected; because those 8 were selected as suspected discordances, they are excluded from the agreement statistics, which derive from the random sample alone. All discordances in both samples were machine under-calls, so the reported detection rates are conservative. Adjudicated labels superseded algorithmic labels. Validation rested on one adjudicator, so interobserver agreement could not be estimated.
