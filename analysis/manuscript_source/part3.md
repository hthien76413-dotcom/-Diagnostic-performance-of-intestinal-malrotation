#H2 Between-modality models, presented as descriptions of practice

#N In the GEE model using all 410 children with at least one index test (740 examinations; Table 4), CT and ultrasound had lower odds of a positive report than the UGI series (odds ratio [OR] 0.31 [95% CI 0.22–0.44] and 0.33 [0.21–0.50]; both p<0.001), essentially unchanged after adjustment for era and age category (CT OR 0.29 [0.21–0.42]; ultrasound OR 0.27 [0.17–0.43]). Detection was higher in the later era (OR 1.79 [1.20–2.68], p=0.004) and in children older than 1 year relative to neonates (OR 3.14 [1.71–5.75], p<0.001), with no difference between infants aged 29 days to 1 year and neonates (OR 1.09 [0.69–1.73]). These coefficients absorb the indication for the test, its position in the diagnostic pathway and the content of the examination, and cannot be separated from them in these data.

#H2 The selected subgroup who received all three examinations

#N Fifty-nine children underwent all three modalities preoperatively (Table 5, Fig. 2). This subgroup is not a random sample and is not a head-to-head accuracy comparison: these were children in whom the diagnosis remained uncertain after the first and second examinations, and they were more severely and more recently affected than the rest (96.6% volvulus vs 86.6% of the other imaged children; 83.1% neonates vs 65.5%; 62.7% operated in the later era vs 31.3%). The question they answer is narrow — among children investigated intensively enough to receive all three tests, which examination named the diagnosis most often — and we do not present it as confirmation of a population-level ordering.

#N Within the subgroup, the UGI series was positive in 45/59 (76.3%), ultrasound in 31/59 (52.5%) and CT in 28/59 (47.5%) (Cochran's Q p=0.001; exact McNemar UGI vs CT p=0.002, UGI vs ultrasound p=0.009, CT vs ultrasound p=0.65). The three examinations were separated by a median of 0.9 days (IQR 0.6–1.6); 47 children (79.7%) had all three within 48 h and 31 (52.5%) within 24 h. Because active volvulus and its imaging signs can evolve over such an interval, the analysis was repeated in these tighter windows: within 48 h detection was 83.0%, 59.6% and 48.9% for the UGI series, ultrasound and CT (Q p<0.001; UGI vs CT p=0.002, UGI vs ultrasound p=0.013), and within 24 h 87.1%, 64.5% and 51.6% (Q p=0.002; UGI vs CT p=0.003, UGI vs ultrasound p=0.039). Restricting to near-simultaneous examinations did not weaken the pattern, but it does not remove the selection that defines the subgroup.

#H2 Detection changed over time, and the change tracked examination content

#N Detection rose across the study period for the two modalities whose technique changed. Ultrasound detection was 41.0% (16/39) in 2012–2018 and 61.3% (49/80) in 2019–2026; CT detection was 47.0% (101/215) and 66.7% (70/105); UGI detection was 77.1% (158/205) and 82.3% (79/96) (Table 6).

The proportion of ultrasound index examinations recorded as abdominal great-vessel studies rose from 28.2% to 62.5% (Fisher p<0.001), while the proportion recorded as gastrointestinal ultrasound fell from 74.4% to 48.8%; documentation of a whirlpool rose from 41.0% to 53.8% and of the mesenteric-vessel relationship from 5.1% to 10.0%. #H1 Discussion

#N In 13.5 years of routine practice, ultrasound reports for children who proved to have intestinal malrotation almost never contained the findings on which modern ultrasound accuracy depends. The third portion of the duodenum and the duodenojejunal junction were documented in none of 119 examinations, the mesenteric-vessel relationship in 8.4%, enteric fluid in one. What the reports did contain was the whirlpool sign, in about half, and detection followed it almost perfectly — 100% when a whirlpool was recorded, 10% when it was not. At this institution, ultrasound for malrotation functioned as a test for midgut volvulus: a child with malrotation whose midgut was not actively twisted at the moment of scanning was very unlikely to be identified.

#N This reframes the most-cited number in our data. Ultrasound named malrotation in 54.6% of surgically confirmed children who underwent it, against published sensitivities of 93–97% in protocolised series and a pooled 94% in meta-analysis [8, 9, 12, 19]. It would be wrong to read that contrast as evidence that ultrasound performs poorly. The published figures were obtained by examinations that assessed D3 and the duodenojejunal junction, often with enteric fluid, by readers alert to the diagnosis [12, 18, 20, 21]; ours were obtained by examinations that, on the evidence of their own reports, did not. The 2021 systematic review anticipated exactly this, finding substantial heterogeneity between studies and better performance where the mesenteric vessels and enteric fluid were used [8]. Our data are a quantified instance of that heterogeneity, with the mechanism visible in the record. An element assessed but not recorded cannot be identified in report text; but a diagnostic element absent from all 119 reports is unlikely to have been routine, and it was certainly not available to the clinician reading the report.

#N The temporal analysis points the same way. Ultrasound detection improved from 41% to 61% between eras, and the improvement was not a property of calendar time: once the shift toward a mesenteric-vessel-focused examination was taken into account, the era effect fell to a non-significant OR of 1.56, while the examination type itself carried an OR of 3.46, and detection with a vessel-focused study was about 70% in both eras. The institution improved because the examination changed, not because time passed. This is the most actionable finding here, because it identifies a modifiable cause, and it suggests that the remaining gap to 93–97% is plausibly attributable to the elements that still never appeared in the reports — D3, the duodenojejunal junction and enteric fluid [20, 21]. Whether adding them would close the gap is a hypothesis this design cannot test, and the counter-argument that D3 position alone cannot support a confident diagnosis deserves weight [22].

#N The same content-dependence held for the other modalities. CT reports describing a mesenteric whirl were positive in 98.3% of cases and those without in 27.9%; the association of contrast enhancement with detection (84.6% vs 47.4%) is consistent with vascular opacification being what makes the whirl visible, although enhanced studies were selected by indication and were more common in older children, so this contrast is confounded and hypothesis-generating. For that reason we have removed the enhanced-CT comparison from the abstract: a confounded subgroup contrast does not belong among a study's headline results. For the UGI series, the duodenojejunal junction was described in 54.5% of reports and jejunal position in 70.4% — the only modality whose routine reports documented the landmark that defines malrotation in the majority of examinations. That, rather than any intrinsic superiority demonstrated here, is the most economical explanation of its higher detection rate.

#N Three features of the design constrain how the between-modality differences may be read, and we regard them as decisive rather than as caveats. First, the reference standard is not independent of the index tests. The operating surgeon knew the preoperative imaging, and a positive UGI series often precipitated the operation that then confirmed the diagnosis, whereas an incidental CT or ultrasound finding was less determinative. This incorporation bias is asymmetrical between modalities and inflates the apparent advantage of whichever test drove the decision to operate; whether surgeons could have been influenced toward operating by a positive UGI series is, in our view, answered in the affirmative — that is how a confirmatory test functions in a diagnostic pathway — and it is a reason why these data cannot rank the tests. Second, position in the pathway differed: the UGI series was the last examination before operation in 75% of children who received it, and its detection was higher in that position (80.4% vs 70.5%). Third, the modalities were used in different children for different reasons (Table 1), and adjustment for the few variables recoverable from records cannot substitute for randomisation. Clinical indications were not recorded prospectively in a form permitting full adjustment, so we have displayed the differences between the groups rather than claiming to have controlled them. For the same reasons the 59 children who received all three examinations are presented as a selected subgroup — 97% with volvulus, 83% neonates, 63% from the later era, assembled by the very diagnostic uncertainty that led to repeated testing — and no longer as confirmation of a population-level ordering of sensitivity, even though restricting to examinations within 48 h or 24 h, which addresses evolution of volvulus between studies, did not weaken the pattern.

#N What, then, is this study for? Not for ranking modalities, and not for resolving the ultrasound-first debate. Its value is that it measures something rarely measured: the distance between diagnostic performance as established in the literature and as delivered by a real department's reports over more than a decade, together with a mechanism for that distance that is visible in the record and open to correction. Departments considering an ultrasound-first pathway on the strength of sensitivities of 93–97% should first establish that their own examinations resemble the examinations that produced those figures — that D3 and the duodenojejunal junction are sought and reported, that enteric fluid is used when needed, and that reports say so. A structured malrotation ultrasound report requiring explicit statements about D3, the duodenojejunal junction, the vessel relationship and study adequacy is a low-cost intervention that would make such a deficit visible prospectively rather than 13 years later. Where such an examination cannot be delivered, a negative ultrasound should not be taken to exclude malrotation in a child with persistent clinical suspicion, and a UGI series remains appropriate. A positive whirlpool sign, highly specific for volvulus in prior work [7, 12] rather than in this cohort, should prompt urgent surgical consultation; our data speak to what was reported, not to the outcome of acting on it.

#H2 Limitations

#N This is not a diagnostic accuracy study and must not be read as one. The cohort was assembled on surgically confirmed disease, so there are no test-negative children and specificity, predictive values and any accuracy-based ranking cannot be estimated; the detection rates apply to children who proved to have malrotation and do not transfer to children investigated for suspected malrotation, in whom contemporary studies report far higher ultrasound performance [8, 12, 19].

#N The cohort is heavily skewed toward severe neonatal disease (65% neonates, 87% volvulus, all operated) and therefore says very little about older children with intermittent symptoms, incidental or non-volvulus malrotation, equivocal presentations or stable outpatients. The six children without volvulus who underwent ultrasound cannot support any inference about ultrasound in uncomplicated malrotation, and the corresponding interaction model does not converge.

#N The reference standard was not independent of the index tests, and modality choice, test sequence and the decision to operate were clinically entangled. Single-modality rates were computed on different, indication-selected denominators; the paired subgroup mitigates but does not remove this, being itself selected for diagnostic uncertainty. The 55 children without any index test were excluded from those denominators; they had been imaged in other ways and often diagnosed elsewhere, but differed systematically from the rest, so their exclusion is informative missingness of unknown direction.

#N Index tests were classified from routine report text by a rule-based algorithm rather than blinded image review. Agreement with a blinded surgeon adjudicator was 92% (kappa 0.83) in a 24-report random sample, but validation rested on one adjudicator and a limited sample, so interobserver agreement could not be estimated; the algorithm and its failure modes are given in Online Resource 1. Correspondingly, this study measures reported detection, not achievable image-based accuracy: a finding seen but not written, and an inadequate examination reported as normal, are indistinguishable here. We regard this as the boundary of the question rather than a defect in answering it, since the report is what the clinician acts on, but it means our figures cannot be attributed to the imaging examination alone.

#N Tentative wording was counted as positive. Because every child had confirmed malrotation no such report was a false positive, and the surgical author team confirmed each individually; the frequency is now tabulated by modality and certainty tier (Table 2), and the principal analyses repeated with possible-tier conclusions reclassified as negative, without change to the pattern.

#N Technique and reporting evolved over 13.5 years, so pooled estimates blend two practice eras; we have therefore reported the eras separately and modelled the change. Comparisons were not adjusted for multiplicity, and the content and subgroup analyses are exploratory. Case ascertainment and the reference standard derived from operative-record text rather than independent re-adjudication, and the operative diagnosis was a clinical label rather than standardised anatomical criteria, so thresholds for borderline anomalies may have varied between surgeons.

#N Because CT was ascertained from archived report text rather than a structured dose registry, per-examination CTDIvol, dose–length product and acquisition parameters could not be captured; this precludes any assessment of radiation burden and falls short of current expectations for reporting CT dose in paediatric imaging research. Prospective comparative studies should capture these metrics.

#N Finally, this is a single-centre experience, and the report-content deficit we describe is a finding about one department's practice. Whether it generalises is unknown and is, in our view, the question most worth asking next: the same audit is inexpensive to repeat wherever routine reports are stored electronically.

#H1 Conclusion

#N In a 13.5-year single-centre cohort of children with surgically confirmed intestinal malrotation, routine ultrasound reports essentially never documented the duodenal landmarks that underpin contemporary ultrasound accuracy, and detection tracked the presence of a whirlpool sign almost perfectly — ultrasound in this department was, in practice, a test for midgut volvulus rather than for malrotation. The improvement in ultrasound detection over time was explained by a shift toward mesenteric-vessel-focused examination rather than by the passage of time. These are report-level detection rates in children with confirmed disease; they do not establish sensitivity, specificity or any ranking of ultrasound, CT and the upper gastrointestinal series, and they should not be used to argue against ultrasound-first pathways. They argue instead that a department's own examination technique and reporting content determine whether published ultrasound performance is achievable locally, and that this is measurable, and correctable, by audit.
