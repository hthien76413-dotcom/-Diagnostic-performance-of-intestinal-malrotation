# Routine Ultrasound Reports for Pediatric Intestinal Malrotation Rarely Document Duodenal Landmarks: A Single-Center Audit

[Masked manuscript. Author names, affiliations and corresponding-author details are provided on the separate title page.]

Word count: 2,995 (Introduction through Take-Home Points; excluding abstract, references, tables and figure legends); Tables: 4; Figures: 3; Supplemental material: Supplements 1–4


## Abstract

Objective: Ultrasound is increasingly advocated as the first-line test for suspected intestinal malrotation, based on reported sensitivities of 93–97%. We audited what routine reports documented and whether they named the diagnosis.

Methods: Retrospective case-only report audit at one tertiary children's hospital (December 2012–June 2026). The preoperative examination closest to operation was classified by a rule-based algorithm with blinded surgical adjudication, and separately audited for documented technical content (ultrasound by two independent readers). Because the cohort was surgically confirmed, the outcome is report-level detection among tested children, with Wilson 95% CIs; specificity, predictive values and comparative accuracy are not estimable.

Results: Among 450 children (volvulus in 88.2%), 398 underwent at least one index test. Three of 117 ultrasound examinations (2.6%, 95% CI 0.9–7.3) documented the third portion of the duodenum or the duodenojejunal junction, 3 (2.6%) the mesenteric artery–vein relationship and 2 (1.7%) enteric fluid; a whirlpool sign was reported in 50.4%. Ultrasound named malrotation or volvulus in 64 of 117 examinations (54.7%, 45.7–63.4): in all 59 recording a whirlpool, itself a positivity criterion, and in 5 of 58 without one. A whirlpool was recorded in 59 of 112 children with operatively confirmed volvulus. Detection was 53.8% counting only conclusions naming malrotation, 25.6% excluding tentative wording.

Discussion: Positive routine ultrasound reports rested almost entirely on the whirlpool sign, and the duodenal landmarks to which published performance is attributed were rarely documented. Documentation, unlike accuracy, can be audited without a reference standard; we provide the criteria as a minimum reporting dataset.

Keywords: Intestinal malrotation; Intestinal volvulus; Infant, Newborn; Ultrasonography; Radiology report


## Introduction

Intestinal malrotation predisposes to midgut volvulus, which can progress within hours to bowel necrosis [1] and lead to short-bowel syndrome or death [1, 2]. Bilious vomiting in a neonate is a surgical emergency until malrotation with volvulus has been excluded, although older children may present atypically and be diagnosed late [3, 4].

The upper gastrointestinal (UGI) contrast series has long been the reference imaging test, based on the position of the duodenojejunal junction and the course of the proximal small bowel [5–7], despite well-described mimics [8]. Abdominal CT is not a dedicated test, and the mesenteric swirl it may show is neither reliably present nor specific in children [9, 10]. The field has moved toward ultrasound: series report sensitivities of 93–97%, a systematic review of 17 studies and 2,257 children estimated a pooled sensitivity of 94% [11–13], and a single-center study of 539 children supported ultrasound as a safe first-line test [14]. This performance is attributed to specific technical elements rather than to the modality itself: the third portion of the duodenum (D3), the duodenojejunal junction, the superior mesenteric artery–vein relationship, enteric fluid and dynamic graded compression [12, 13, 15–17]. The same literature records these elements as demanding and not universally achievable, and D3 position alone as insufficient for diagnosis [18]. The 2020 ACR Appropriateness Criteria, which predate most of this evidence, rate ultrasound as "may be appropriate" and the UGI series as "usually appropriate" for bilious vomiting in infants older than 2 days [19].

The 2025 multicenter series measured performance from the original clinical reports, after excluding non-diagnostic and equivocal examinations [13]. A report audit of the UGI series for malrotation found discrepancies with blinded re-reading and proposed a structured template [20], but whether routine reports from a general pediatric ultrasound service document the elements to which that performance is attributed, and how this relates to whether the diagnosis is reported, has not to our knowledge been examined at scale.

We therefore audited 13.6 years of routine reports at a tertiary children's hospital. Because every child had surgically confirmed malrotation, the study measures how often the disease was reported and with what documented content, not how accurate the tests are. The UGI series and CT are reported alongside ultrasound to show where ultrasound sat in the diagnostic pathway, not to rank them. The audit criteria are provided as a minimum reporting dataset that other departments can apply (Supplement 4).


## Methods


### Design, terminology and estimable quantities

This was a retrospective, single-center, case-only report audit approved by the institutional review board with waiver of consent. Reporting follows STROBE [21]; STARD was not used, as the design is not that of a diagnostic accuracy study. The cohort includes no children in whom malrotation was suspected and excluded. The outcome is the report-level detection rate among surgically confirmed children who underwent the test. We avoid the term "sensitivity", which applies only within the indication-selected group that underwent each test and does not transfer to children investigated for suspicion. Specificity, predictive values and any accuracy-based ranking cannot be estimated. The whirlpool sign is a sign of volvulus rather than of malrotation [22].


### Participants, reference standard and index tests

The institutional clinical research database supplied the records of 711 children with a recorded diagnosis of intestinal malrotation between December 2012 and June 2026, including 559 operative records in 499 children. Every operative record was read, and operations whose operative diagnosis named intestinal malrotation or whose procedure was recorded as a Ladd procedure were selected (search strings in Supplement 1): 503 records in 465 children. The anchor operation was each child's first such operation. One author, with the imaging labels visible, re-read all operative records for 34 children whose anchor operation was not named as a malrotation procedure or followed earlier malrotation surgery: 19 were retained and 15 excluded, 6 in whom that operation did not confirm malrotation and 9 reoperations (Supplement 1). The remaining 450 consecutive children form the reference standard. Midgut volvulus was defined by an explicit operative statement of torsion or by a documented degree of midgut or mesenteric rotation, with no minimum degree required (see Limitations). No sample-size calculation was performed.

Three modalities were evaluated: ultrasound (sessions booked as gastrointestinal, abdominal great-vessel or pyloric), abdominal CT and the UGI series; contrast enemas were not counted. For each child and modality, the index examination was the examination episode closest to operation, pooling every report of that modality issued that day, as the department routinely issues separate gastrointestinal and great-vessel reports for one ultrasound session. Operative times were recorded as dates only, so the 154 same-day reports and the 27 index examinations more than 7 days before operation were reviewed individually; three issued after the operation or for an unrelated earlier illness were removed. This yielded 723 index examinations from 761 reports; 32 earlier repeat examinations were not audited (Figure 1). The clinical indication on each request was not retrievable, so confounding by indication is displayed through the characteristics of each modality group (Table 1) rather than adjusted for.

Ultrasound was reported by ultrasound physicians, and CT and the UGI series by radiologists, during routine care, unaware of the operative findings but not blinded to clinical history or previous imaging, and not by a designated reader group. Reports were free text, with no structured malrotation template in use. The department reports no change in ultrasound equipment, reporting staff or protocol during the study period.


### Report-content audit and classification

Each ultrasound index examination was coded by two readers, an ultrasound physician and a pediatric surgeon, both authors, working independently from the findings and conclusion text against written criteria for twelve elements, from D3 and the duodenojejunal junction to the whirlpool sign and limiting bowel gas (Table 3). Technique elements were counted as documented whether the finding was normal or abnormal; the whirlpool sign, a finding, only where described as present. Agreement was assessed with Cohen kappa and disagreements adjudicated. Booking category and bedside performance were taken from the examination name, and CT and UGI landmarks were coded by text patterns (Supplement 1).

An examination was classified as positive when its conclusion named malrotation or midgut volvulus, including tentative phrasing, or described a modality-specific sign: for ultrasound, a whirlpool or an explicit statement of mesenteric vessel inversion; for the UGI series, a corkscrew or spring appearance or a malpositioned duodenojejunal junction; for CT, a mesenteric whirl or an abnormal duodenal position. Explicit negation was classified as negative, and isolated gastric volvulus was not counted. Because the whirlpool is both an audited element and a positivity criterion, and a conclusion may name volvulus without malrotation, detection was also computed counting only conclusions that named malrotation. Tentative phrasing is standard here, so the certainty tier of every positive conclusion was tabulated and the analyses repeated with "possible" tiers counted as negative.

Report text was classified using a rule-based, clause-level algorithm with explicit negation handling [23] (Supplement 1). A pediatric surgeon, one of the authors, blinded to the operative findings adjudicated 32 reports: agreement was 92% (Cohen kappa 0.83, 95% CI 0.61–1.00) in a stratified random sample of 24, and a targeted review of 8 suspected discordances found 5 further under-calls, excluded from the agreement statistics. A two-directional screen of every index examination then returned nine positives that no diagnostic term or sign pattern supported, three of which source review confirmed as over-calls. All errors were corrected; because adjudication read the whole report, the final label is the algorithmic label after review, not the conclusion alone. With a single adjudicator, interobserver agreement on the detection label could not be estimated.


### Statistical analysis

Detection rates are presented with denominators and Wilson 95% confidence intervals (CIs). All analysis variables were complete. Between-modality comparison used a generalized estimating equation (GEE) logistic model [24] with an exchangeable working correlation, patient-level clustering and cluster-robust standard errors, unadjusted and adjusted for era (2012–2018 vs 2019–2026) and age group. Modalities a child did not undergo are unobserved outcomes, not missing at random here, so these coefficients describe reporting among children who underwent each test. A pre-specified modality-by-volvulus interaction was not estimable, no ultrasound examination being positive among the five children without volvulus; a Firth penalized estimate is given in Supplement 2 [25, 26]. In the subgroup that underwent all three modalities, detection was compared using the Cochran Q test, the exact McNemar test and paired differences with bootstrap CIs (Supplement 2). The temporal change was explored, after inspection of the data, using modality-specific logistic models of detection on era before and after adding one covariate (booking category or contrast enhancement), reported as odds ratios and average marginal effects (Table 4); the era boundary was varied from 2019 to 2022 and era-by-covariate interactions fitted. Attenuation was not interpreted as mediation. P values were not adjusted for multiplicity; Supplement 2 lists which analyses were pre-specified. Analyses used Python 3.11 (statsmodels 0.15, SciPy 1.17).


## Results


### Cohort and children without an index test

Of 450 children with surgically confirmed malrotation, 300 (66.7%) were neonates and 397 (88.2%) had midgut volvulus (Table 1). An index examination was available in 293 children for the UGI series, 313 for CT and 117 for ultrasound; 398 underwent at least one and 59 all three. The remaining 52 (11.6%) underwent none of the three, but all had other preoperative imaging; in four it was documented before transfer (Table 1, Figure 1).

The modality groups were not interchangeable (Table 1). Ultrasound was performed more often in children with volvulus, its use rising from 13% to 51% of operated children between eras. Among children who underwent more than one index test, the UGI series was the last preoperative test in 75.0% of those who received it against 19.2% for CT, and detection was higher in that position (80.0% [144/180] vs 70.0% [42/60] for the UGI series), a feature of the pathway rather than of the test.


### Detection and certainty of wording

Detection was 230/293 (78.5%, 95% CI 73.4–82.8) for the UGI series, 165/313 (52.7%, 47.2–58.2) for CT and 64/117 (54.7%, 45.7–63.4) for ultrasound (Table 2, Figure 2). In a GEE model adjusted for era and age group, CT and ultrasound remained less often positive than the UGI series; the coefficients absorb indication, pathway position and examination content and are given in Supplement 2. Reclassifying possible-tier conclusions as negative reduced detection to 50.2%, 32.9% and 25.6%; counting only conclusions that named malrotation gave 77.8%, 48.6% and 53.8% (Table 2).


### Documented content of ultrasound examinations

Of the 117 ultrasound index examinations, three (2.6%; 95% CI 0.9–7.3) documented D3 or the duodenojejunal junction and seven (6.0%) mentioned the duodenum (Table 3, Figure 3). Three (2.6%) stated the mesenteric artery–vein relationship, and only one stated that the vessels were inverted, one of the two ultrasound positivity criteria. Enteric fluid was recorded twice and graded compression once; a whirlpool was reported in 59 examinations (50.4%) and bowel gas limited 36 (30.8%). Reader agreement was 1,423 of 1,428 item codes in the 119 episodes read (kappa 0.80–1.00; Supplement 1).

Of these three, only one, in 2022, followed D3 between the artery and the aorta and identified the duodenojejunal junction, as the published technique describes [16, 17].

The diagnosis was named in all 59 examinations recording a whirlpool and in 5 of the 58 without one (8.6%, 95% CI 3.7–18.6). This contrast is partly definitional, as a whirlpool is itself a positivity criterion, although all 59 conclusions also named malrotation explicitly. A whirlpool was recorded in only 59 of the 112 children (52.7%) in whom operation confirmed midgut volvulus.


### Change in detection over time

In this exploratory analysis, ultrasound detection was 42.1% (16/38) in 2012–2018 and 60.8% (48/79) in 2019–2026, a difference of +18.7 percentage points whose 95% CI (−0.2 to +37.7) includes zero; CT detection rose and UGI detection did not (Table 4). Sessions including a great-vessel study rose from 34.2% to 68.4%, and adding this booking indicator reduced the ultrasound difference to +8.4 (−10.6 to +29.2) percentage points (Supplement 2). Whirlpool reporting rose from 39.5% to 55.7%, whereas D3 or the duodenojejunal junction was documented in one and two examinations.


### Selected subgroup and populations not represented

Fifty-nine children underwent all three modalities. This subgroup, in whom the diagnosis remained uncertain, was more severely affected than the other 339 imaged children (volvulus 96.6% vs 87.9%; neonates 83.1% vs 67.3%). The UGI series was positive in 76.3%, ultrasound in 52.5% and CT in 45.8% (Cochran Q test, P < .001; UGI series minus CT +30.5 percentage points, 95% CI +13.6 to +47.5; CT minus ultrasound −6.8, −20.3 to +8.5), a pattern unchanged when all three examinations fell within 48 h (Supplement 2). Only 5 of the 53 children with malrotation but no volvulus underwent ultrasound, and none had a positive report; a Firth penalized model supports the direction of this contrast but not its magnitude (Supplement 2).


## Discussion

Over 13.6 years, routine ultrasound reports for children who proved to have intestinal malrotation rarely recorded the duodenal anatomy to which contemporary ultrasound accuracy is attributed: D3 or the duodenojejunal junction in three of 117 examinations, enteric fluid in two. The finding they did record was the whirlpool sign, present in about half of all examinations and of the children in whom operation confirmed volvulus. Such a gap might be expected with free-text reports and no condition-specific protocol, as here; the audit shows its size and its consequence: a positive report rested almost entirely on a sign of volvulus.

Malrotation or volvulus was named in 54.7% of the surgically confirmed children who underwent ultrasound, compared with 93% in the original clinical reports and 97% on blinded review in the 2025 multicenter series, which excluded non-diagnostic and equivocal examinations [13] (in a multi-institutional cohort, 25.7% of examinations for malrotation were non-diagnostic on the original report [27]), and a pooled 94% in meta-analysis [11], both in children investigated for suspicion. These figures are not interchangeable: ours is conditioned on confirmed disease and on the indications that selected these children, theirs on those exclusions, so the difference combines case mix, examination quality and technique in proportions this audit cannot separate. What it does show is that the elements to which published performance is attributed were almost never documented here: D3 [16, 17], the most accurate single sign in the 2025 series [13]; the duodenojejunal junction [12, 15]; and enteric fluid, associated with better performance in meta-analysis [11]. Whether documenting them would have raised detection cannot be tested, as documentation is a lower bound on what was done.

The temporal analysis is exploratory: the crude rise was imprecise, the booking indicator was chosen after inspection of the data, and booking was not randomized, as a child suspected of volvulus is both more likely to be booked for a vessel study and more likely to have a whirlpool. The consistent observation is descriptive: duodenal landmarks were documented no more often in the later era, while whirlpool reporting rose.

Three features of the design make the between-modality differences uninterpretable as accuracy. The reference standard is not independent of the index tests: the surgeon knew the preoperative imaging, and a positive UGI series, the last preoperative test in 75.0% of children who had it with another index test, may often have precipitated the operation that confirmed the diagnosis, an incorporation bias asymmetrical between tests. Pathway position therefore also differed. The modalities were also used in different children for different reasons (Table 1), which no adjustment can undo.

The documentation gap bears on departments adopting an ultrasound-first pathway on the basis of sensitivities of 93–97%, and on guidance weighing that evidence [19]: published performance applies only where examinations resemble those that produced it. Unlike accuracy, documentation needs no reference standard and can be audited prospectively in every examination requested for suspected malrotation. Supplement 4 sets out the audited elements as a minimum reporting dataset, with this audit as the baseline for a second cycle. These data cannot estimate the negative predictive value of a routine report, but a department whose reports lack these elements should not assume that published values apply to its own.


### Limitations

This is not a diagnostic accuracy study: detection among surgically confirmed children who underwent a test does not transfer to children investigated for suspicion [11, 13, 14]. The cohort contains only children who reached an operation, is weighted toward severe neonatal disease, and provides little information on older children with intermittent symptoms, incidental or non-volvulus malrotation, or stable outpatients. The 52 children without an index test were excluded from the denominators, an informative missingness, as were 9 whose operation followed earlier malrotation surgery. Excluding children whose operative record states a rotation below 360° changes prevalence from 88.2% to 84.0% without altering any conclusion (Supplement 2).

Index tests were classified from report text rather than blinded image review, so the study measures reported detection rather than achievable image-based accuracy; a finding seen but not recorded and an inadequate examination reported as normal cannot be distinguished, although the report is what clinicians act on. The certainty tiers were derived from the same text and, unlike the positive–negative label, were not separately adjudicated. Radiation dose could not be captured. This is one audit cycle at one center: whether the deficit generalizes, whether a structured report would raise documentation and whether documentation would raise detection are unknown.


## Take-Home Points

• Routine ultrasound reports for children with surgically confirmed malrotation documented the duodenal landmarks emphasized by published series in 2.6% of examinations and reported the diagnosis almost only when a whirlpool sign was present.

• A positive routine ultrasound report rested almost entirely on the whirlpool sign, a sign of volvulus, which was recorded in only 59 of 112 children with operatively confirmed volvulus.

• These are report-level detection rates among surgically confirmed children, not sensitivities; they establish no specificity, predictive value or ranking of modalities, and are no argument against ultrasound-first pathways.

• Documentation, unlike accuracy, can be audited without a reference standard; the audit criteria are provided as a minimum reporting dataset, with this audit as the baseline for re-audit.
